Why Councils Across England Have Stopped Taking Your Old Fridge

Fridges and freezers have become the hardest item in the house to get rid of. Across England and Wales, a growing number of local authorities have suspended or restricted the collection of cooling appliances at household waste recycling centres (HWRCs) and through bulky waste services. Several councils are now describing it as a national crisis in specialist recycling capacity.

Warwickshire County Council became the latest to act, suspending all fridge and freezer collections at its recycling centres with immediate effect on 5 August 2026. It joined Carmarthenshire, which stopped accepting cooling appliances on 13 July after its sites reached their safe storage limits, and Nottinghamshire, which introduced a similar suspension the same week. Ceredigion has banned fridges and freezers from its Glanyrafon site in Aberystwyth, Norfolk was forced to restrict several electrical waste streams earlier in the year, and South Gloucestershire, Peterborough and parts of Hampshire have all reported disruption to collections.

Districts and boroughs in each affected county have mostly honoured bookings already made, but new requests are being turned away, and commercial deliveries of fridges and freezers have been paused in some areas too.

Why the network has collapsed

Fridges and freezers sit in an unusually fragile part of the UK's waste infrastructure. Unlike paper, metal or plastic, cooling appliances cannot simply be diverted to the next available recycler when a plant goes offline. They contain refrigerant gases and insulating foam that must be removed under tightly controlled conditions to stop ozone-depleting and greenhouse gases escaping, and that work can only happen at a small number of Approved Authorised Treatment Facilities (AATFs) built specifically for the job.

Three events in under a year have knocked a large chunk of that capacity out of action. In November 2025, Unimetals Recycling, which had bought Sims Metal Management's UK operations for £195 million just a year earlier, filed for compulsory liquidation. Its Newport site was one of only a handful of specialist fridge recycling plants in the country, and its closure is estimated to have removed around 600,000 units of annual treatment capacity from the market in one go.

The sector then lost more ground in quick succession. In February 2026, a fire closed EMR's fridge recycling plant at Darlaston in the West Midlands. Two months later, a fire severely damaged Wiser Recycling's treatment facility at Thetford, which had handled a large share of Norfolk's electrical waste. Wiser later lost its AATF status and stopped operating from the site altogether, removing another outlet from an already thin network.

For councils, the immediate problem is not collecting fridges. It is storing them. HWRCs have permit conditions that cap how much hazardous waste they can hold on site at any time. As treatment throughput has fallen, storage compounds have filled up, and once they hit their limit, the only option left is to turn people away.

What is being done

The Environment Agency and the recycling industry have set up a working group to manage the storage bottleneck while EMR's Darlaston site comes back online and Enva completes a new 124,000 sq ft fridge recycling plant in Dartford, Kent. The Agency has also approved a Regulatory Position Statement allowing businesses to apply for permission to store fridges on unlicensed sites in the meantime, and industry figures are exploring exporting cooling appliances overseas for treatment while UK capacity is rebuilt.

The scale of what the network needs to absorb is considerable. The UK's domestic housing stock alone holds an estimated 37.65 million fridges, freezers and combined units, with an average appliance lifespan of around 12.5 years. That means a steady, predictable stream of replacements every year regardless of how much spare capacity exists to process them. Large household appliances, fridges chief among them, already account for roughly a third of all household electrical waste collected in the UK.

What this means if you handle waste

A fridge turned away at the tip does not stop being hazardous waste. Refrigerant-containing appliances are classed as hazardous under the WEEE Regulations, and the duty of care that applies to anyone disposing of them does not pause just because the local HWRC has run out of space. If you are a business replacing appliances, or a landlord or letting agent clearing a property, you still need a registered waste carrier and a compliant waste transfer note or hazardous waste consignment note for every fridge or freezer that leaves your premises, capacity crisis or not.

With HWRCs turning people away and storage compounds full, the temptation to leave a fridge on the kerb or hand it to whoever will take it cheapest is only going to grow. That is the gap fly-tippers exploit. Checking that a carrier is registered with the Environment Agency and keeping a paper trail for where the appliance actually went is what stands between you and liability if it turns up somewhere it should not.
